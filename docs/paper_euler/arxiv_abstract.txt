Euler's identity $e^{i\pi}=-1$ can be read as a half turn around the circle: it singles out $-1$, the only number other than $1$ whose square is $1$, a switch with two positions. Number theory attaches such plus-or-minus signs to many of its objects, and they often decide something important, such as whether an equation has finitely or infinitely many rational solutions. We argue that a great many of these signs share one source, the contribution of the real numbers (the "real place"), and we trace it through the Langlands program, from the root number of every elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ to the quaternions, the real Weil group and the oddness of modular Galois representations, ending with eight research directions. We then describe Artemis, an open-source laboratory in which AI agents run pre-registered computational experiments that a frozen, fingerprinted program judges, and report its first test. Over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$, which has two real places, so that the two half turns cancel, murmurations of elliptic curves follow $+\prod_{\mathfrak p} w_{\mathfrak p}$, the reverse of the rule over $\mathbb{Q}$, as theory predicts (correlation 0.53, 95% interval [0.46, 0.59]), although the two patterns part company for large primes. Among curves over $\mathbb{Q}$ with the same conductor, the local signs of rank-2 curves differ slightly but certainly from those of rank-0 curves: on about 210,000 curves held out until the end, a rule fixed in advance tells the two ranks apart 52% of the time and the laboratory's rule 54% of the time, against 50% for chance (z = 8.6 and 15.1). The signal lies chiefly in the size of the bad prime. The final test was reproduced exactly, and all code and data are public.
