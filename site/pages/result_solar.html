Summary

The question was which earth-abundant inorganic crystals in NIST JARVIS-DFT a lab should calculate next to find excellent solar absorbers. Every element had to be common in the Earth's crust, and cadmium, mercury, lead, thallium, arsenic and radioactive elements were excluded.

An excellent absorber has a spectroscopic limited maximum efficiency (SLME, a calculated upper limit on solar-cell efficiency) of at least 30 percent. It must also sit no more than 0.1 eV per atom above the convex hull, which measures how far a crystal is from being stable; zero means stable. Both thresholds were fixed before any experiment ran.

SLME needs an expensive calculation, so the lab's job was to rank candidates using only cheap information and reach the excellent ones in as few expensive calculations as possible. The winning method puts near-stable materials first. It then orders them by an SLME predicted from cheap properties and composition, using an ensemble of randomized decision trees trained on materials JARVIS has already assessed.

What the lab tried

The lab stopped by its own rule after three waves without a kept improvement, having used 26 of its 60 allowed evaluations.

What it learned

Stability first. Putting near-stable materials first raised the band-gap rule's acceleration from about 1.7 to about 11 on the practice pool. An excellent absorber must also be stable, and that check is cheap.

Aim the quick band gap near 0.7 eV, not 1.34 eV. JARVIS gives two band gaps: a quick one (the OptB88vdW method) and an accurate one (the TBmBJ method). Every excellent absorber in the training data with a known accurate gap lies between 0.92 and 1.66 eV, with a median of 1.35 eV. Their quick gaps have a median of only 0.66 eV, because the quick method underestimates gaps. Among stable training materials, about half of those with quick gaps between 0.3 and 0.8 eV are excellent absorbers. The share falls to 13 percent between 1.2 and 1.4 eV, and 1 percent above 1.8 eV (recomputed from the data snapshot).

Composition matters beyond the gap. The best window shifts with the non-metal element, and the excellent absorbers cluster in alkali halides and fluorides and in compounds of antimony, indium, silver, copper and zinc. In cross-validation, the learned model roughly doubled what the corrected-gap rules achieved (acceleration about 50 against about 23). Predicting SLME itself worked better than predicting a yes-or-no label.

The final method

Candidates no more than 0.1 eV per atom above the hull go first. Within each group, candidates are ordered by SLME predicted by 500 randomized decision trees (scikit-learn's ExtraTreesRegressor, at least 2 samples per leaf), trained on the 7,250 JARVIS materials with known SLME. For the shortlist the model was retrained on 8,155, adding the practice pool.

The inputs are only what a routine calculation and the chemical formula provide: the quick band gap, formation energy, distance from the hull, number of elements and atoms, space group, density and volume per atom. They also include the mean and spread of Pauling electronegativity, the fractions of chalcogens, heavier halides and oxygen, and the fraction of each element.

The lab's original code was lost when its sandbox closed. The method was rebuilt from this description and matches the lab's practice result exactly: the ninth hit at calculation 13, with average precision 0.899. It also gives 14 of the same 15 recommendations. Read the rebuilt code.

Limits

The labels are computational. SLME is a theoretical efficiency limit from a calculated absorption spectrum. It says nothing about defects, how charge moves through the material, interfaces, or whether the material can be made.

The official practice and test pools were small, with 9 and 4 hits. The robustness check came after the official test, used a rebuilt copy of the method, and its pools overlap with each other and include the test materials. The lab also chose its method partly by looking at the practice pool, whose materials are spread through the robustness pools. The consistency across all 20 pools, and the Skeptic's earlier checks on training data alone, suggest this bias is not large, but they do not rule it out.

The toxicity screen excludes only Cd, Hg, Pb, Tl, As and radioactive elements.
